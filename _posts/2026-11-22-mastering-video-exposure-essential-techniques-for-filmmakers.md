Getting your exposure right is the single most important technical skill you can master as a filmmaker. It’s not about having the best camera; it’s about knowing how to expose for video correctly, no matter what gear you’re holding. A perfectly composed shot with terrible exposure is just a terrible shot. We’re aiming for beautiful, intentional exposure every time.

## The Exposure Triangle: Shutter, Aperture, ISO

You've heard it before, but it's worth reviewing: exposure is controlled by three main settings. Think of them as a team. Change one, and you usually have to adjust another to keep your exposure consistent.

### Shutter Speed

For video, your shutter speed is almost always locked at a specific setting: double your frame rate. Shooting 24 frames per second (fps)? Your shutter speed should be 1/48th of a second. If your camera doesn’t have 1/48th, use 1/50th. Shooting 30fps? Use 1/60th. This "180-degree rule" gives you natural motion blur. Go faster, like 1/250th, and your footage looks choppy and hyper-real, like the Omaha Beach scene in *Saving Private Ryan*. That's an intentional choice for a specific effect, but for most narrative work, stick to 1/48th or 1/50th.

This means shutter speed isn't a primary tool for adjusting exposure in video. It's usually set and forgotten. If you find yourself changing shutter speed to adjust brightness, you’re often doing it wrong for cinematic motion. Get an ND filter instead.

### Aperture (f-stop)

Aperture controls two things: how much light enters the lens and your depth of field. A lower f-number (like f/2.8) means a wider opening, letting in more light and giving you a shallow depth of field (blurry background). A higher f-number (like f/11) means a smaller opening, less light, and more of the scene in focus.

This is your primary creative tool for exposure. Want a creamy bokeh behind your subject? Shoot wide open, like f/2.8 or f/4. Want everything in a wide shot to be sharp? Stop down to f/8 or f/11. When learning how to expose for video correctly, mastering your aperture's relationship to depth of field is key.

### ISO

ISO dictates your camera's sensitivity to light. A lower ISO (e.g., 100 or 400) means less sensitivity and less digital noise. A higher ISO (e.g., 1600 or 3200) means more sensitivity, letting you shoot in darker conditions, but at the cost of introducing grain or noise into your image.

Every camera has a "native ISO" or a "dual native ISO." This is the setting where your sensor performs best, offering the cleanest image with the most dynamic range. For many Sony cameras like the a7S III, it's ISO 640/12800. For Canon C70, it's ISO 800/3200. Find your camera's native ISO and try to stick to it as much as possible. Use ISO to compensate when aperture and ND filters aren't enough, but treat it as your last resort before adding more light.

## Tools for Precise Exposure

Your camera’s LCD screen lies. It's often too bright or too dim depending on your environment. You need objective tools to nail exposure.

### Zebras

Zebras are stripes that appear on parts of your image that are overexposed (or underexposed, depending on your camera's settings). Most cameras let you set a zebra threshold. For example, set your zebras to 100 IRE (a video signal measurement) to show you where highlights are clipping. This helps you protect your brightest areas from blowing out. If you're shooting log footage (like S-Log3 or C-Log), you might set zebras at a lower percentage, say 70 IRE, to ensure skin tones are properly exposed.

**Practical Tip:** When shooting interviews, set your zebras to show clipping at 100 IRE. Then, expose your subject's face so that no zebras appear on their skin. You want their skin to have detail, not be blown out. Use an ND filter or adjust your aperture until the zebras disappear from their face.

### Histograms

A histogram is a graph that shows the distribution of tones in your image, from darks on the left to highlights on the right. A healthy histogram uses the full range without crushing blacks (spiking on the far left) or clipping whites (spiking on the far right).

If your histogram is clustered to the left, your image is underexposed. If it's clustered to the right, it's overexposed. An ideal histogram often has a gentle curve or a central peak, with information across the entire range. It's a great objective way to see if you’re using your sensor’s full dynamic range.

### Waveforms and False Color

These are professional monitoring tools found on higher-end cameras, external monitors, and apps like [FrameCoach](https://framecoach.io).

*   **Waveforms** are like a histogram turned on its side, showing luminosity values across the width of your frame. They let you see the brightness levels of different parts of your scene. You can easily spot if a window in the background is blowing out compared to your subject's face.
*   **False Color** is a fantastic tool that assigns different colors to specific exposure values. For example, mid-grey might be green, overexposed areas red, and underexposed areas blue. This gives you an instant visual readout of your exposure, much more intuitive than zebras for many filmmakers. If you want real-time feedback on your exposure settings while you shoot, [FrameCoach](https://framecoach.io) gives you that coaching layer right on your phone, offering false color and other essential monitoring tools.

These tools are how you truly learn how to expose for video correctly, moving beyond just eyeballing it.

## The Right Way to Use ND Filters

Neutral Density (ND) filters are sunglasses for your camera. They reduce the amount of light entering your lens without affecting color. Since your shutter speed is typically fixed, and you often want to shoot at a specific aperture for depth of field, ND filters become essential.

Imagine shooting outside on a sunny day. You want to shoot at f/2.8 for shallow depth of field, and your shutter is at 1/50th. Without an ND filter, your image would be wildly overexposed. An ND filter lets you bring down the exposure to manageable levels while keeping your desired f-stop and shutter speed.

You'll need a set of ND filters or a variable ND filter. Variable NDs are convenient but can sometimes introduce a "cross-polarization" pattern at their strongest settings. Test yours before a big shoot.

## Exposing for Log Footage

Many modern cameras shoot in "log" profiles (like S-Log, C-Log, V-Log). These profiles record a flat, desaturated image that holds more dynamic range, giving you more flexibility in post-production. However, log footage requires specific exposure techniques.

Log footage is often designed to be slightly *overexposed* on set to "lift" the shadows above the noise floor. For example, Sony's S-Log3 is often recommended to be exposed 1 to 2 stops brighter than "middle grey" would suggest. This is called "exposing to the right" (ETTR). By pushing the exposure brighter, you get cleaner shadows. Then, in post, you bring the overall exposure down during color grading.

Always check your camera manufacturer's recommendations for exposing their specific log profile. Don't just expose log footage to look good on your un-LUTed screen; use a monitoring LUT or false color. This is critical for knowing how to expose for video correctly when working with these advanced profiles.

## Common Exposure Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

1.  **Trusting the Camera's Auto-Exposure:** Never use auto-exposure for narrative or serious documentary work. The camera will constantly adjust, leading to distracting brightness shifts. Always shoot manual.
2.  **Over-Exposing Highlights:** It's much easier to recover detail from slightly underexposed shadows than from blown-out highlights. Protect your brightest areas. If a window is pure white, it's gone forever.
3.  **Underexposing for "Cinematic Look":** While shadows are crucial, underexposing too much, especially with lower-end cameras, can introduce excessive noise. Find the balance. Aim for a rich image that still has detail in the shadows.
4.  **Forgetting ND Filters:** Trying to compensate for bright light by cranking up your f-stop to f/22 or your shutter speed to 1/2000th will ruin your depth of field and motion blur. Always carry ND filters.

Learning how to expose for video correctly means building habits that ensure consistent quality. It means trusting your tools, not just your eyes.
