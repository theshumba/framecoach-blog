Achieving correct video exposure is fundamental to good filmmaking. Get it wrong, and your footage looks either muddy and underexposed or blown out and unusable. Knowing how to expose for video correctly means understanding your camera's exposure triangle: aperture (f-stop), shutter speed, and ISO. Each one controls the light hitting your sensor, but they also affect other aspects of your image, like depth of field and motion blur.

You need to develop an intuition for exposure, not just blindly follow meters. That intuition comes from practice and understanding what each setting does.

## The Exposure Triangle: Your Core Tools

### Aperture (f-stop)
Aperture controls how much light passes through your lens to the sensor. It's measured in f-stops (e.g., f/1.4, f/2.8, f/5.6, f/11). A smaller f-number means a wider aperture, letting in more light and creating a shallower depth of field. A larger f-number means a narrower aperture, letting in less light and creating a deeper depth of field.

For narrative filmmaking, we often chase shallow depth of field to isolate subjects. Think of those beautiful close-ups where the background melts away. You'd typically shoot wide open, like f/2.8 or f/4, depending on your lens and desired look. But this isn't always the right choice. If you're shooting a wide shot of a landscape and want everything in focus, you'd stop down to f/8 or f/11.

**Practical Tip:** When starting a shoot, set your aperture first. Decide your depth of field based on your creative vision. Do you want a blurry background or sharp foreground-to-background? That choice dictates your starting f-stop.

### Shutter Speed
Shutter speed dictates how long your camera's sensor is exposed to light. For video, this isn't just about brightness; it's crucially about motion blur. The standard rule for video is the "180-degree rule," meaning your shutter speed should be double your frame rate.

If you're shooting 24 frames per second (fps) for a cinematic look, your shutter speed should be 1/48th of a second. Since 1/48th isn't always an option on cameras, 1/50th is a common approximation. Shooting 30fps? Go for 1/60th. This creates natural-looking motion blur, similar to what you see in film.

Deviating from the 180-degree rule will give you different effects. A faster shutter speed (e.g., 1/250th or 1/500th) will freeze motion, giving you a staccato, hyper-real look like the battle scenes in *Saving Private Ryan*. A slower shutter speed (e.g., 1/24th with 24fps) will introduce more blur, sometimes used for dreamlike sequences or to imply speed.

**Warning:** Don't use a high shutter speed (like 1/1000th) just to make your image brighter. It will make your video look choppy and unnatural unless that's your specific creative choice. It's a common mistake when learning how to expose for video correctly.

### ISO
ISO controls your sensor's sensitivity to light. A lower ISO (e.g., 100, 400) means less sensitivity and a cleaner image. A higher ISO (e.g., 1600, 3200) means more sensitivity, making your image brighter but introducing digital noise or grain. Every camera has a "native ISO" or a range of native ISOs where it performs best with the least amount of noise. For many modern cameras like the Sony a7S III or Blackmagic Pocket 6K, these are often around ISO 800 or 1250, sometimes even dual native ISOs.

Always try to keep your ISO as low as possible to maintain image quality. Only raise it when your aperture and shutter speed are set, and you still need more light. Think of ISO as your last resort for exposure. Pushing ISO too high, especially in darker areas of your image, will quickly reveal noise, which can be tough to clean up in post-production.

## Practical Steps to Expose for Video Correctly

Here’s a workflow I use on set:

1.  **Set Your Frame Rate:** This is usually dictated by your project (24fps for film, 30fps for broadcast, higher for slow-motion). Lock this in first.
2.  **Set Your Shutter Speed:** Apply the 180-degree rule. If you're at 24fps, set it to 1/50th. If 30fps, 1/60th.
3.  **Set Your Aperture:** Based on your creative vision for depth of field. Do you want f/2.8 for a shallow look or f/8 for deep focus?
4.  **Adjust ISO (Last):** Now, with your aperture and shutter speed locked, adjust your ISO to get the correct exposure. Aim for the lowest ISO that gives you a bright, but not overexposed, image. If you're outdoors and it's too bright even at ISO 100 and a high f-stop, you'll need an ND filter.

If you’re still struggling to get a clean image at a low ISO because of insufficient light, don't just crank the ISO. That's when you need to add more light to your scene or reconsider your shooting location/time of day. No amount of ISO will make a truly dark scene look good without a lot of noise.

## Understanding and Using Exposure Tools

Your camera has built-in tools to help you determine correct exposure. Don't rely solely on your eye, especially on a bright set or a small monitor.

### Histograms
A histogram is a graph that shows the distribution of tones in your image, from pure black (left) to pure white (right). A well-exposed image will generally have a bell-shaped curve, with information spread across the mid-tones.

-   **Peaks on the left:** Your image is underexposed, with too many dark tones.
-   **Peaks on the right:** Your image is overexposed, with too many bright tones, likely clipping highlights.
-   **Gaps on the edges:** You're losing information in the shadows or highlights.

Learn to read your histogram. It’s an objective measure of light in your frame. A tool like [FrameCoach](https://framecoach.io) can give you a clear, real-time histogram right on your phone, which is incredibly useful for getting consistent exposure across takes. Sometimes on a run-and-gun shoot, you're looking at a small camera screen in direct sunlight. The histogram becomes your best friend.

### Zebras
Zebras are stripes that appear on areas of your image that reach a certain brightness level. You can usually set the threshold for zebras (e.g., 70 IRE for skin tones, 100 IRE for highlights).

-   **Skin Tone Zebras (70-75 IRE):** A good starting point for Caucasian skin tones is to have zebras just *starting* to appear on the brightest parts of the skin. This ensures your talent is well-exposed without being blown out.
-   **Highlight Clipping Zebras (95-100 IRE):** Set your zebras to 95% or 100% to indicate areas that are completely overexposed and losing all detail. Avoid seeing these zebras on important parts of your image like faces or key elements in the scene.

### False Color
False color modes assign different colors to different exposure values in your image, giving you a heat map of your scene's brightness. For example, green might represent perfectly exposed mid-tones, blue for underexposed areas, and red for overexposed areas. Different cameras and external monitors have varying false color scales, so familiarize yourself with yours.

False color is one of the most powerful tools for quickly evaluating your exposure across the entire frame. If you're shooting log footage, false color is almost essential because log looks flat and underexposed to the naked eye, making it hard to judge exposure correctly. This is another area where [FrameCoach](https://framecoach.io) provides invaluable real-time feedback, showing you exactly where your exposure stands across the entire scene.

## Shooting Log Footage and Exposure

Modern cameras often offer "log" picture profiles (e.g., Sony S-Log, Canon C-Log, Panasonic V-Log). These profiles capture a much wider dynamic range, meaning they retain more detail in both the shadows and highlights. The trade-off is that log footage looks flat, desaturated, and often underexposed straight out of the camera. It requires color grading in post-production.

When shooting log, you often need to expose a bit brighter than you normally would – sometimes a stop or two over what a standard meter might tell you. This is called "exposing to the right" (ETTR) on the histogram, meaning you push the mid-tones and highlights further to the right without clipping. This brings more information into the brighter, cleaner part of your sensor's response curve, which can reduce noise in shadows after grading.

It's critical to know your camera's log profile and how it prefers to be exposed. For example, S-Log3 on Sony cameras often benefits from being exposed 1-2 stops over "nominal" to achieve cleaner shadows. If you don't expose log footage properly, you'll end up with noisy shadows or blown-out highlights that are unrecoverable. So, when learning how to expose for video correctly, especially with log, practice and testing are key.

## Don't Forget ND Filters

ND (Neutral Density) filters are essentially sunglasses for your lens. They reduce the amount of light entering your camera without affecting color or depth of field. They are crucial for shooting outdoors in bright conditions when you want to maintain a wide aperture (for shallow depth of field) and your correct 180-degree shutter speed.

If you're shooting at f/2.8, 1/50th shutter, and ISO 100 on a sunny day, your image will be completely overexposed without an ND filter. A variable ND filter gives you flexibility to dial in the exact light reduction you need. Having a good set of ND filters is just as important as having good lenses when you're serious about mastering how to expose for video correctly.

## The Final Check: Monitor Calibration and Scene Assessment

Even with all the tools, the ultimate judge of your exposure is your calibrated monitor. If your on-camera screen or external monitor isn't showing you an accurate image, all your efforts with histograms and zebras might still lead you astray. Calibrate your monitor periodically.

Beyond the technical, always assess your scene. What is the brightest part? What is the darkest? Where do you want your audience's eye to go? Your exposure decisions should always serve the story and the visual intention. Sometimes, intentionally underexposing a background element or overexposing a light source can create a specific mood. But this is a *controlled* decision, not an accident.

Mastering video exposure takes time and practice. Get out there and shoot. Experiment with different light conditions. Look at your footage critically in post-production to see where you succeeded and where you missed the mark. Each shoot teaches you something new about how to expose for video correctly.

For your next project, set your frame rate, lock your shutter to the 180-degree rule, decide your depth of field, and then dial in your ISO. Use your histogram and zebras, and if you’re shooting log, consider false color or a monitoring app like [FrameCoach](https://framecoach.io) to ensure you're getting the right amount of light on your sensor. You’ll see a significant improvement in the quality and consistency of your footage.
